The Arbitrator Wanted Him Reinstated. The City Manager Said No — And Won.

Legal News · Collective Bargaining · July 21, 2026

Legal News

Advisory arbitration is exactly what it sounds like — advisory — and a published California Court of Appeal decision just confirmed that a city manager who disagrees with an arbitrator's findings can reject them entirely and still have the final word. In Ramirez v. City of Indio, No. D082997 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 13, 2024), the court held that when a Memorandum of Understanding vests the city manager with authority to affirm, revoke, or modify an arbitrator's advisory recommendations, the city manager may evaluate the totality of an employee's conduct and reach a different conclusion than the arbitrator did, even on credibility.

The case began with a police officer's termination after an internal affairs investigation and pre-disciplinary conference. The chief found the officer had driven under the influence of alcohol, based on his own admissions, and separately found dishonesty and poor judgment that embarrassed the department. The officer appealed through the MOU's advisory arbitration procedure. The arbitrator sided with the officer, finding the department failed to prove the DUI, crediting the officer's explanation that resetting his department-issued phone was standard practice rather than evidence tampering, and finding his testimony "credible" regarding inconsistencies between his internal affairs statements and later trial testimony. The arbitrator recommended reinstatement.

The city manager rejected that recommendation outright. Reviewing the same record, the city manager agreed with the chief's original findings: the officer had driven under the influence, reset his phone with intent to prevent discovery of incriminating information, and made dishonest or inconsistent statements throughout the process. The city manager also considered aspects of the officer's conduct the arbitrator had found irrelevant, upholding the termination based on the totality of the circumstances. The officer petitioned for a writ of mandate, arguing the city manager was bound to defer to the arbitrator's credibility findings — and lost, both at the trial court and on appeal.

The Court of Appeal's reasoning matters well beyond this one case: due process requires a fair hearing and a genuine opportunity to respond, not a guarantee that any particular decision-maker's findings will control the outcome. Where an MOU's appeals procedure explicitly gives final authority to someone other than the arbitrator, that decision-maker is not bound by the arbitrator's view of the facts, even on issues of witness credibility. For officers relying on a favorable arbitration recommendation as the end of the road, this case is a reminder to read your MOU's appeals language closely — advisory arbitration means exactly that, and the person with final say may see the same testimony very differently than the arbitrator did.
