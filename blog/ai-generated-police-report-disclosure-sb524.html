If Your Officer's Report Was Written by AI, the Law Now Says You Have to Say So

Legal News · Discipline · June 30, 2026

Legal News

Departments experimenting with AI tools that turn body camera footage or dictated notes into a finished report now have a new compliance layer to build into that workflow, thanks to Senate Bill 524. The law does not ban AI-assisted report writing, but it treats the practice as something that has to be visible, traceable, and ultimately owned by a human being — not quietly absorbed into an officer's normal paperwork routine.

Under SB 524, any report drafted fully or partly with AI must carry a disclosure on every page identifying the specific AI program used and stating plainly that the document was written with artificial intelligence. The officer who prepared it still has to sign it, personally vouching that they reviewed the content and that the facts stated are true — meaning an officer can't treat an AI draft as a black box they simply approve without reading. The law also requires agencies to retain every version of the report, including earlier AI-generated drafts, for as long as the final version is kept, and it flatly states that an AI-generated draft does not count as the officer's own statement for legal purposes.

The audit trail requirement is where this gets operationally serious. The AI software itself must log who used it to generate the report, who edited it afterward, and what underlying video or audio was fed into it. Vendors supplying AI report-writing tools to a department are barred from selling or otherwise using the agency's data outside narrow exceptions, closing off a data-privacy gap that would otherwise come baked into any commercial AI contract. Agencies had to have a compliant policy in place by January 1, 2026, meaning any department that rolled out AI report-writing tools without pairing them with a written policy is already out of step with the statute.

For officers, the practical lesson is that AI-assisted drafting doesn't lower your personal exposure if the report turns out to be wrong — you're still the one certifying it, and the retained draft history means any material difference between the AI's first pass and your final signed version is now a permanent, discoverable record. Treat the review step as substantive, not a formality, because a future cross-examination is far more likely to ask about it than it used to be.
