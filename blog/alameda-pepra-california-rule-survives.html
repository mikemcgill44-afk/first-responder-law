The California Supreme Court Just Decided How Much of Your Pension Promise Is Actually Safe

Legal News · Pensions & Retirement · July 30, 2020

Legal News

When the Legislature passed the Public Employees' Pension Reform Act of 2012, it narrowed what counts as "compensation earnable" for calculating pension benefits under the County Employees Retirement Law of 1937 — closing off certain pay add-ons and premiums that had previously boosted final pension calculations. Deputy sheriffs and other "legacy" members hired before PEPRA took effect sued, arguing the change unconstitutionally impaired the vested pension rights they'd already earned. In Alameda County Deputy Sheriffs' Assn. v. Alameda County Employees' Retirement Assn. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1032, the California Supreme Court disagreed and upheld PEPRA's changes.

The case turned on California's long-standing "California Rule," which generally protects public employees' vested pension rights from being reduced without a comparable new advantage in exchange. The Court didn't abandon that rule, but it clarified its real boundaries: legislative changes that disadvantage employees can still survive if they're closely related to the theory of a pension system and are enacted to prevent abuse — like closing loopholes that let certain pay items inflate a pension beyond what the underlying retirement system was actually designed to fund. The Court found PEPRA's compensation-earnable changes fell within that category, even though legacy members lost real value as a result.

For rank-and-file law enforcement and fire personnel, the decision is a reminder that "vested rights" in a pension is not the same as "frozen rights." The California Rule still bars the state from simply cutting benefits to save money, but it does not bar closing loopholes or tightening definitions the Legislature believes were being exploited — even where legacy members end up with a smaller number on their final pension calculation than they expected when they were hired. Anyone whose final compensation depended on special pay items curtailed by PEPRA should understand this ruling before assuming those add-ons will count toward their pension the way older members' once did.
