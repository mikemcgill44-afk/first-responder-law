Disciplined AND Retaliated Against? Why Losing One Fight Doesn't Mean You Lose the War

Here's a scenario that trips up a lot of employees and, frankly, a lot of employers too: a state personnel board upholds your suspension as justified on the merits. Case closed, right? Not necessarily. A California appellate court just confirmed that a completely separate track — a grievance arbitration alleging the same suspension was actually retaliation for union activity — can still succeed, run its own course, and result in real relief, even after the personnel board has already ruled against you on the underlying discipline.

A Bulletin Board Posting, a Suspension, and Two Very Different Questions

A correctional officer and union job steward received discipline after posting excerpts of her own disciplinary paperwork on a union bulletin board, an act her department called a violation tied to promoting a "code of silence." The state personnel board upheld a lengthy suspension on that basis. Separately, she filed a grievance arguing the suspension actually violated her contract's protections against retaliation for protected union activity. That grievance went to arbitration, and the arbitrator reached a very different conclusion: the department had retaliated against her for protected speech and representational activity, the posting wasn't so far over the line that it lost protection, and the department never proved it would have imposed the same suspension anyway. The arbitrator ordered the discipline rescinded and full back pay restored.

The department went to court, arguing the arbitrator had no business second-guessing what the personnel board had already decided. A trial court agreed and struck the arbitrator's remedy. The union appealed, and the appellate court reversed — reviving the arbitrator's award in full.

Two Tracks, Two Questions, No Actual Conflict

The appellate court's reasoning turns on a distinction that's easy to miss but matters enormously: the personnel board asked whether the discipline was supported by cause. The arbitrator asked whether that same discipline was imposed in retaliation for protected activity. Those questions overlap on the facts but are legally distinct, and the personnel board never actually addressed the retaliation question at all. Because enforcing the arbitrator's award didn't require overturning or contradicting anything the personnel board actually decided, there was no conflict for a court to police. The arbitrator wasn't vetoing the board's ruling — she was offsetting it with a separate contractual remedy for a separate violation.

The court applied the same logic to the department's other argument, that the award undermined public policy against a "code of silence" among officers. Important as that policy is, it doesn't dictate a specific length of suspension, and it doesn't stop an arbitrator from finding that a particular disciplinary decision was actually driven by retaliation rather than legitimate cause.

Why This Matters Even If You've Already Lost Your Disciplinary Appeal

If you've been through a disciplinary appeal and lost, that outcome doesn't automatically close the door on a separate retaliation claim through your union — whether in grievance arbitration or before the state's labor relations board. These really are separate legal questions, and a loss on one doesn't decide the other. What it does mean is that building and preserving your evidence of retaliatory motive — timing that looks suspicious, disparate treatment compared to colleagues, communications suggesting animus toward your union activity — matters throughout the entire disciplinary process, not just at the personnel board stage.

A Word of Caution for POST Decertification Cases

One important wrinkle: this reasoning doesn't map cleanly onto POST decertification proceedings. Those proceedings ask essentially the same underlying misconduct question as a disciplinary appeal, just under a tougher evidentiary standard, which means a sustained finding elsewhere can carry more weight there than it did in this case. If you're navigating both a disciplinary matter and a potential decertification issue at the same time, the interaction between them deserves careful, individualized attention.
