Your Department Can No Longer Keep Seized Property Without a Conviction — Here's the Current State of California Forfeiture Law

Legal News · Criminal Law · June 4, 2025

Legal News

California's landmark 2016 forfeiture reform, codified through amendments to Health and Safety Code section 11470 et seq., remains the operative framework departments navigate when handling seized cash and property tied to drug investigations. The reform requires a criminal conviction before most state-law forfeitures involving property or cash of $40,000 or less can proceed — a significant departure from the lower civil standard many departments previously relied on.

The reform also closed a federal workaround that had let departments sidestep the stricter state standard entirely: state and local agencies can no longer refer or transfer seized property to a federal agency for adoption under looser federal forfeiture rules, cutting off the equitable-sharing pipeline that had generated hundreds of millions of dollars for California agencies over the prior decade.

California's civil forfeiture laws currently earn a C grade from independent legal-reform trackers as of 2025 — an improvement from years past, but still reflecting ongoing concerns about the gap between seizure and any judicial finding of wrongdoing. For officers making seizure decisions in the field, that gap is exactly where legal exposure and evidentiary scrutiny concentrate.

Departments still running seizure programs built around the pre-2017 rules should audit their current practices against the conviction requirement. What was routine a decade ago can now generate a viable civil claim if the underlying conviction never materializes.
