An Isolated Act of Dishonesty Doesn't Have to End Your Career — And Now There's a Published Case to Prove It

Legal News · Discipline & Decertification · August 10, 2026

Legal News

Departments love a simple rule: if a dishonesty charge sticks, the officer is gone. It's clean, it's easy to defend on appeal, and it saves the agency the messy work of actually weighing what happened. A California appellate court just ruled that this simple rule is wrong.

The case is California Highway Patrol v. California State Personnel Board, decided by the Second Appellate District on July 29, 2026, and ordered published on August 5 — meaning it's now binding precedent that officers and their advocates can cite anytime "dishonesty" gets treated as an automatic death sentence.

Here's what actually happened. CHP Officer Nathaniel Partridge had worked for the department since 2006 without a single prior disciplinary mark, pulling strong performance reviews the whole way. He was assigned to overtime enforcement details in highway construction zones — work Caltrans paid the department to cover. Local policy said that if Caltrans cut a shift short, the officer had to stay at the station rather than leave early. One day in September 2017, Partridge didn't. He went home, and the department still paid him for the roughly three hours and forty-five minutes of overtime he was scheduled for but never worked. Separately, over a six-month stretch, he'd missed logging his patrol vehicle's return time eight times.

CHP called it dishonesty and fired him. The State Personnel Board didn't dispute the facts, but it looked at the whole picture — one incident, no history of misconduct, years of solid service — and knocked the penalty down to a year-long suspension instead of termination.

CHP fought back, arguing dishonesty is simply a "continuing trait of character" and that once it's found, the officer has to go, period. The Court of Appeal shut that down. The cases agencies lean on to demand automatic termination, the court pointed out, involve officers who lied to cover up things like inmate abuse, or whose dishonesty was tangled up with far worse misconduct. A missed log entry and an early departure from a slow overtime shift isn't in that league. Degrees of dishonesty exist under the law, whether or not an agency wants to admit it.

The ruling doesn't stop at the discipline hearing, either — it reaches straight into POST decertification proceedings under SB 2. POST can revoke certification for serious misconduct including dishonesty, but "may" isn't "must." The regulations call for an individualized look at whether the conduct was material, whether it was willful, and whether it fits a pattern. Those are exactly the factors that saved Partridge's badge. An isolated slip and a documented pattern of lying are not the same thing, and the law was never supposed to treat them that way.

If you're facing a dishonesty charge built on one incident, and your record is otherwise clean, this case just became one of the strongest tools available to push back. It's published. It's binding. And it says plainly: one bad day doesn't have to end a career.
