The City Picked the Arbitration Process — Then Tried to Walk It Back After Losing

Legal News · Collective Bargaining · July 15, 2026

Legal News

A sheriff's department that directs an officer's disciplinary appeal down one procedural path cannot switch to a more favorable one after that path produces a result it doesn't like — at least not according to a California Court of Appeal decision built entirely on the doctrine of equitable estoppel. The case is a cautionary tale for any agency juggling overlapping appeal procedures between a civil service rule and a collective bargaining agreement.

The San Francisco Sheriff's Department terminated a deputy sheriff for misconduct, and the termination notice itself told him his appeal was governed by Rule 122 of the city's Civil Service Commission Rules. The parties selected a neutral arbitrator under that rule, and the arbitrator ultimately reduced the termination to a written reprimand, ordering the deputy reinstated with back pay and full benefits. Two years later, a newly appointed sheriff informed the deputy she would not accept that outcome, pointing instead to the deputy sheriffs' collective bargaining agreement — a document that made arbitration decisions non-binding on the department whenever the recommendation reduced discipline, and that required disciplinary grievances to follow an entirely different appeals track than Rule 122.

The deputy and his association sued to enforce the arbitration award, arguing the department was equitably estopped from now relying on the CBA after having directed the appeal under Rule 122 in the first place. The trial court agreed and confirmed the award; the Court of Appeal affirmed. Equitable estoppel against a government entity requires five elements — the entity knew the true facts, intended its conduct to be relied upon, the other party was ignorant of the true facts, that party relied on the conduct to its detriment, and injustice would result without estoppel — and the court found all five satisfied. The department had followed Rule 122 for two decades, the deputy had no reason to know the CBA's contrary process even applied, and the department had participated in arbitration without objection for two full years before invoking the CBA only after losing.

The court called this exactly the kind of injustice equitable estoppel exists to prevent: an employer cannot select an appeal process, let an officer and his association rely on it in good faith, and then retroactively deny it applied once the outcome goes against the agency. For associations and represented officers, the case is a reminder to document precisely which appeals procedure a department invokes at the start of any disciplinary matter — because if the agency later tries to change the rules after an unfavorable result, that paper trail is exactly what a court will look to first.
