California Can't Punish You for Filing a 'False' Complaint Against an Officer Anymore — Here's Why That Cuts Both Ways

Legal News · Criminal Law · November 18, 2025

Legal News

In Los Angeles Police Protective League v. City of Los Angeles (Cal. 2025), the California Supreme Court struck down a decades-old rule that made it a crime to knowingly file a false misconduct complaint against a police officer — and required citizens to sign a warning to that effect before their complaint could even be accepted. The Court found both pieces unconstitutional, ruling that singling out complaints against officers for this kind of speech-chilling treatment violates the First Amendment.

The statute at issue, Penal Code section 148.6, required every citizen complaint form to carry an advisory: sign here acknowledging that a knowingly false statement could result in criminal prosecution. Civil liberties groups had argued for years that the warning deterred legitimate complaints as much as false ones, since most people have no way to predict in advance how an internal affairs investigation will ultimately characterize their account.

For departments, the ruling changes intake procedures immediately: agencies can no longer require complainants to sign any advisory referencing criminal liability, and citizen complaint forms need updating to drop the language entirely. Some departments had leaned on the advisory as a deterrent against frivolous or retaliatory complaints filed against officers involved in controversial incidents.

The double-edged nature of this ruling is worth sitting with. Fewer procedural hurdles for citizens filing complaints could mean more complaints reaching Brady/Giglio disclosure discussions and internal affairs files — which matters for officers navigating promotion, reassignment, or litigation down the line. Knowing the rules just changed is the first step in managing what comes next.
