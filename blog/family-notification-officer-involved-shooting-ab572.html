Before Detectives Can Question a Grieving Family, California Now Makes Them Show ID First

Legal News · Civil Rights · June 9, 2026

Legal News

Investigating an in-custody death or officer-involved shooting almost always means eventually sitting down with the immediate family of the person who was killed or seriously injured, and starting January 1, 2027, California law will require that conversation to start very differently than it often has in the past. Assembly Bill 572 imposes a mandatory identification and disclosure protocol on any peace officer or prosecuting attorney who initiates a formal interview with that family for investigative purposes.

Before the interview begins, the officer or prosecutor must clearly state their full name and employing agency, and for in-person interviews, physically display official identification such as a badge or business card. They also have to inform the family member of the injured or deceased person's status, to the extent it's known, and explicitly tell the family that the interview may or may not involve scrutiny of the deceased or injured person's own conduct — removing the ambiguity that has historically left grieving families unsure whether they were being treated as witnesses or as subjects of an inquiry into their loved one's behavior.

The law also gives family members a right to bring a trusted support person into the interview, someone who needs no special certification but cannot be a witness to, or suspect in, the underlying incident. AB 572 separately bars officers and prosecutors from using threats or deception to coerce cooperation during these interviews. The requirements fall away only where a reasonable officer believes delay risks losing evidence or creates an imminent public-safety threat, or where the family member has already received Miranda-equivalent advisements.

For agencies, this means building a documented pre-interview script into critical-incident protocols well before the January 2027 deadline, not treating it as a courtesy left to individual investigators' discretion. For officers conducting these interviews, the law effectively converts what used to be an informal opening exchange into a series of affirmative disclosures that will now show up in any later review of how the interview was conducted.
