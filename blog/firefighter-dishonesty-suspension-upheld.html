He Lied About Being Late — And Turned a Possible Termination Into a 15-Day Suspension

Legal News · Discipline · June 5, 2026

Legal News

A firefighter who was late to a scheduled shift made a decision that turned a routine tardiness reprimand into a dishonesty investigation that could have ended his career — and a California trial court's rejection of his subsequent writ challenge shows just how much deference a public employer's disciplinary decision gets once dishonesty enters the picture. The firefighter's discipline was reviewed through a writ of administrative mandamus, the specific procedural vehicle California law provides for challenging a public employer's disciplinary decision in civil court.

The firefighter arrived late to his shift, not for the first time, and his commander verbally reprimanded him for it — the kind of routine correction that in most cases ends there. Instead, the firefighter lied about why he was late and disputed whether there was even a record of his late arrival at all. That response triggered a formal investigation, which substantiated both an instance of dishonesty and separate performance issues. Because the dishonesty finding alone could have supported termination, the firefighter was fortunate the department chose instead to impose a 15-day suspension.

He challenged the suspension anyway, filing a petition for a writ of administrative mandamus asking a state civil court to review whether the underlying disciplinary decision was lawful and whether the punishment was justified — the two questions that kind of writ exists to answer. The court sided with the department on both, finding it had correctly determined the underlying facts and that a 15-day suspension was well within its discretion given a sustained dishonesty finding that could have justified termination outright.

The larger point for firefighters and officers alike: once an internal investigation substantiates dishonesty, the disciplinary conversation is no longer really about the original, minor infraction — it's about whether termination is warranted, and any lesser punishment a department chooses to impose instead will be very hard to overturn on appeal. If you're being questioned about a minor policy violation, resist the instinct to smooth over the details; a dishonesty finding turns a survivable conversation into a career-ending one far more often than the original infraction ever would have on its own.
