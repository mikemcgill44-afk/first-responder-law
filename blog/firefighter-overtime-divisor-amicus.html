How Your Overtime Rate Gets Calculated Just Became a Ninth Circuit Fight — And Firefighters Have Skin in the Game

Legal News · Collective Bargaining · April 27, 2026

Legal News

David Barnett et al. v. City of San Jose (N.D. Cal., on appeal to the Ninth Circuit) is exactly why. If you've ever tried to explain your own paycheck to a non-firefighter and watched their eyes glaze over at "224-hour work period," you already understand why this case matters more than it sounds like it should.

David Barnett et al. v. City of San Jose is now in front of the Ninth Circuit, and it centers on one deceptively simple question: when you calculate a salaried firefighter's regular rate of pay for FLSA overtime purposes, do you divide by the fixed 224-hour schedule the department actually pays for, or by whatever hours the firefighter happened to work that period? Judge James Donato said it's the former — the fixed divisor under 29 C.F.R. Section 778.113(a) — because the salary is meant to cover a set 112-hour-per-28-day schedule, not a moving target.

That call matters because the City's alternative method would let the regular rate swing lower whenever firefighters, thanks to their 48/96 schedule and the inevitable unscheduled hours, worked more than average. Lower regular rate means lower overtime pay, forever, as a matter of routine math rather than any actual negotiation.

Judge Donato also ruled that holiday-in-lieu payments — the extra pay firefighters get for working a job that doesn't take holidays off — have to be folded into the regular rate calculation, not carved out as if they were pay for time off. That's consistent with a published decision the plaintiffs' own firm won years earlier against Richmond, so this isn't exactly uncharted water.

California Professional Firefighters has now filed an amicus brief backing the ruling on appeal, and with good reason: more than 32,000 career firefighters statewide have overtime checks that ride on how this divisor question gets resolved. If the Ninth Circuit affirms, agencies lose a quiet way to shave overtime costs off the books without ever sitting down at the bargaining table.
