A City Told Firefighters Their Union Couldn't Talk Staffing With Elected Officials. The Union Sued.

Legal News · Meet & Confer · August 6, 2026

Legal News

Meet-and-confer rights are usually thought of as something that happens inside a negotiating room. A recent lawsuit is a reminder that the underlying free-speech interests at stake don't stop existing the moment a union representative walks out that door and talks to an elected official instead.

Firefighters in Kansas City, Kansas, and their union filed suit against the Unified Government of Wyandotte County and Kansas City, Kansas, alleging their First Amendment rights were violated when the union was prohibited from discussing apparatus staffing levels and firefighter safety concerns directly with elected officials. The complaint frames this as government suppression of union speech on matters that are inherently public — staffing levels and safety conditions affecting both firefighters and the community they serve are about as textbook "matter of public concern" as it gets.

The case isn't a California dispute, but the underlying tension is one that shows up constantly here too: agencies that treat the formal meet-and-confer process as the only legitimate channel for labor communication, and try to muzzle anything that happens outside it — union advocacy directly to a city council, a board of supervisors, or other elected decision-makers. That instinct runs headlong into the reality that public employees and their unions retain independent First Amendment rights to advocate on public safety issues, separate and apart from whatever statutory bargaining obligations exist.

For California fire unions watching this litigation, the practical lesson is straightforward: statutory meet-and-confer rights are a floor, not a ceiling, on your ability to advocate for staffing and safety. An agency policy that tries to silence union communication with elected officials outside the bargaining table is standing on thin constitutional ice.
