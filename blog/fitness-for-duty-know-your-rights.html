Told to Report for a Fitness-for-Duty Exam? Know Exactly What the Department Can and Can't Ask

Legal News · Fitness for Duty · July 8, 2026

Legal News

A fitness-for-duty referral can feel like it comes out of nowhere — one day you're working your normal shift, the next you're being told to report for a psychological evaluation before you can go back on patrol. It doesn't have to feel like a black box, though, because the rules governing when and how these evaluations can happen are actually pretty specific.

Under Government Code section 1031(f), peace officer candidates and, by extension, working officers subject to fitness inquiries must be evaluated against a standard tied to whether a condition might adversely affect the exercise of law enforcement powers — not a general, open-ended "we're concerned about you" standard. For active employees, any employer-mandated medical or psychological examination has to be job-related and consistent with business necessity, a standard that requires more than a supervisor's vague unease.

The screening process itself isn't unregulated either. Title 2 of the California Code of Regulations, section 599.975, sets standards and procedures for psychological screening, and departments that skip or shortcut those procedures are exposing themselves to exactly the kind of challenge that gets a fitness-for-duty finding thrown out.

If you're referred for an evaluation, ask — in writing — what specific job-related concern triggered the referral, and confirm the evaluator is applying the standards the regulations actually require. A fitness-for-duty finding built on a shortcut process is a finding that can be challenged, and knowing the rules up front is the difference between a fair evaluation and a rubber-stamped outcome.
