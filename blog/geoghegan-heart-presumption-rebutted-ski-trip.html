He Had a Heart Attack Skiing in Colorado — Why His Firefighter Pension Claim Still Failed

Legal News · Line-of-Duty Disability · July 25, 1990

Legal News

Patrick Geoghegan, a 49-year-old San Francisco firefighter, suffered a heart attack while on a ski trip in Vail, Colorado — six days into the trip, with no history of heart problems and a treadmill test he'd recently passed. He applied for industrial disability retirement, and San Francisco's charter gave him a presumption that heart trouble developing while a firefighter is in service is service-connected. The Retirement Board denied the claim anyway, and in Geoghegan v. Retirement Board of the City and County of San Francisco (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1525, the Court of Appeal upheld that denial.

The presumption of service connection for heart trouble is one of the most valuable protections firefighters and police officers have — it shifts the burden to the employer to prove the condition came from somewhere else. But Geoghegan's own treating cardiologist testified that the cold and high altitude in Colorado most likely triggered the coronary artery spasm that caused his myocardial infarction, and that but for the ski trip, the heart attack probably wouldn't have happened when it did. That was enough specific, non-industrial causation evidence to rebut the presumption, even though Geoghegan argued his underlying predisposition to coronary spasms — not the skiing — was the real cause.

The court also rejected Geoghegan's argument that a favorable workers' compensation award for the same heart condition should have bound the Retirement Board through collateral estoppel. Because the retirement system is an independently funded, independently governed entity separate from the city agency that paid the workers' comp claim, the two proceedings aren't treated as involving the same parties — a distinction that has tripped up more than one applicant who assumed winning one case meant automatically winning the other.

The lesson for firefighters and officers relying on a heart-trouble or similar statutory presumption is that the presumption is a starting point, not a guarantee. If there's a specific, well-documented non-industrial trigger — a ski trip, a second job, an unrelated medical event — the retirement board can and will use it to rebut the presumption, and a workers' compensation win on the same facts won't carry over automatically to a separate disability retirement proceeding.
