California Just Made It Easier to Enforce Red Flag Orders — Here's What Changed for Officers in the Field

Legal News · Criminal Law · April 2, 2026

Legal News

Senate Bill 899 tightens the enforcement mechanics behind California's decade-old gun violence restraining order (GVRO) law, streamlining how courts compel firearm surrender from individuals subject to an order and closing gaps that had let some respondents delay compliance.

The change comes alongside a March 2026 release from the Attorney General's office: a Model GVRO Policy for California law enforcement agencies, updated legal guidance, a ten-year implementation report, and a comprehensive field guide covering when officers should consider requesting a GVRO and how to document, serve, and enforce all three order types — emergency, temporary, and long-term.

The numbers show how much this tool has scaled since 2015: longer-term GVROs issued annually more than doubled between 2021 and 2024, with California courts issuing GVROs against 1,727 individuals in 2024 alone. Officers responding to domestic disturbance or crisis calls increasingly have this as a distinct legal pathway separate from criminal charging.

For officers unfamiliar with the current GVRO framework, the Attorney General's model policy is worth a careful read — it lays out concrete criteria for when a Firearms Emergency Protective Order is appropriate versus when the situation calls for the longer-term court process, and gets into the documentation standards that hold up when a respondent challenges the order later.
