Win the Federal Case, and the State Wrongful Death Suit Can Die With It

Legal News · Wrongful Death · May 28, 2009

Legal News

George Hernandez led Pomona police officers on a car chase, crashed, and fled on foot before officers shot and killed him during the pursuit. His family sued in federal court under civil rights law, arguing the officers used excessive deadly force, and lost — a federal jury and court found the shooting objectively reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. The family then filed a second lawsuit in state court, this time claiming the officers' decisions leading up to the shooting were independently negligent. In Hernandez v. City of Pomona (2009) 46 Cal.4th 501, the California Supreme Court held the second lawsuit was barred.

The case turned on collateral estoppel — the rule that once an issue has been fully and fairly litigated and decided, the losing party can't relitigate it in a later case. The plaintiffs tried to sidestep this by framing their state claim around the officers' "pre-shooting" tactical decisions rather than the shooting itself, arguing that was a distinct issue never actually decided in the federal case. The California Supreme Court rejected that framing, holding that the federal judgment on the reasonableness of the officers' conduct necessarily encompassed the events leading up to the shooting, not just the trigger pull itself.

The practical effect is significant for departments and officers who successfully defend a civil rights excessive-force claim in federal court: that victory can do more than resolve the federal case — it can foreclose a follow-on state-court negligence or wrongful death theory built around the same incident, even if the second lawsuit is dressed up to look like a different legal argument. Plaintiffs' attorneys have adapted since Hernandez by pleading state and federal claims together from the outset specifically to avoid this trap, which is itself useful context for understanding how officer-involved-shooting litigation gets structured today.
