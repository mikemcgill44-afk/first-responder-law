Your K9's Bite File Just Got a Lot More Public — Here's the Case That Did It

Legal News · Civil Litigation · May 26, 2026

Legal News

A Fifth Appellate District ruling on K9 bite-record disclosure (Cal. Ct. App., 5th Dist., 2026) just torched that strategy. Every K9 handler already knows the drill: a bite happens, a report gets written, and somewhere down the line someone asks whether that report is public. For years, agencies leaned on a narrow reading of "great bodily injury" to keep most bite files out of reach. A Fifth Appellate District panel just torched that strategy.

The ruling reinterprets Penal Code section 832.7 — the statute that pulls certain categories of peace officer records out of confidentiality and into the daylight of a public records request — and lands on a broader definition of what counts as "significant or substantial physical injury" for a canine-inflicted wound. Punctures, lacerations, and the kind of tissue damage that used to get waved off as routine dog-bite trauma now have a real shot at clearing the bar.

The practical effect lands on K9 units first. If a bite report crosses the GBI threshold under this reading, the agency has to treat it the way it would treat any other use-of-force record subject to disclosure — meaning redaction protocols, response deadlines, and the very real possibility that a reporter or advocacy group gets the file.

This isn't just a records-clerk problem. Handlers should assume that any bite with meaningful tissue damage is getting logged as if it will eventually be read by someone outside the department, because under this decision, it probably will be. That changes how incident narratives get written — precision matters more than ever, because a sloppy report doesn't stay internal anymore.

Agencies that have been quietly categorizing K9 bites as minor to sidestep 832.7 disclosure now have a published appellate decision working against that instinct. Time to review the classification policy before a records request does it for you.
