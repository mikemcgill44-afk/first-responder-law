Who Actually Controls Your Retirement Board? The Supreme Court Just Answered

Legal News · County Retirement · August 3, 2026

Legal News

In a split 4-3 decision, the California Supreme Court ruled in Los Angeles County Employees Retirement Assn. v. County of Los Angeles that county retirement boards operating under the County Employees Retirement Law of 1937 (CERL) — including the board that administers LACERA, the system covering Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department deputies and other county safety members — do not have final constitutional or statutory authority to set job classifications and salaries for the employees who run the retirement system itself. That authority stays with the county board of supervisors.

The dispute started in 2018, when LA County declined to adopt salary amendments LACERA's board had proposed for its own staff, arguing the Board of Supervisors — not the retirement board — controlled compensation decisions for those positions. LACERA disagreed, relying on Proposition 162's grant of "plenary authority" to retirement boards over system administration. After the County again rejected LACERA's 2021 classification and salary requests, LACERA sued for a writ of mandate. The Court of Appeal sided with LACERA, calling its authority "complete and absolute" as to system management. The Supreme Court reversed.

The Court held that Proposition 162's plenary authority language protects a retirement board's control over investing system assets and delivering member benefits and services — not a blank check to set employment classifications or salaries for retirement-system staff, who remain county employees under the county's civil service rules. The justices did leave retirement boards a real remedy: if a county unreasonably withholds approval of a board's staffing recommendations, the board can still go to court and challenge that delay for abuse of discretion.

For rank-and-file LACERA members, the case isn't about your own pension check — it's about who has the final word on how the agency managing your benefits is staffed and run. The Court's framing of "cooperative responsibility" between the county and the retirement board means governance fights like this one are likely to keep surfacing, and members with a stake in how well their retirement system is administered should pay attention to who's actually setting the rules.
