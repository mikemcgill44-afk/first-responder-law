Your Department Can't Interrogate You Without This Warning — The Case That Made It Law

Legal News · Discipline · December 31, 1985

Legal News

Michael Lybarger was a Los Angeles police officer assigned to a vice unit when internal affairs opened a major investigation into his squad. When investigators ordered him to answer questions or face discipline for insubordination, nobody told him that his compelled answers couldn't later be used against him in a criminal case. He talked, was later criminally charged based partly on what he said, and was fired. In Lybarger v. City of Los Angeles (1985) 40 Cal.3d 822, the California Supreme Court held that his termination was improper because he was never given that critical protection before he was forced to speak.

The California Supreme Court's holding, construing the Public Safety Officers Procedural Bill of Rights Act, established what's now known simply as a "Lybarger warning": before compelling an officer to answer questions in an administrative interrogation under threat of discipline, the department must advise the officer that the statements — and any evidence gained from them — cannot be used against the officer in a subsequent criminal proceeding. Without that warning and that immunity, an officer who might otherwise have remained silent and accepted the consequences is deprived of a real, informed choice.

The Court's reasoning turned on fairness: an officer facing an order to talk or lose their job is in a fundamentally different position than a private citizen facing police questioning. If the price of keeping the job is answering questions that could also be used to prosecute you criminally, the "choice" to cooperate isn't really voluntary unless the department first guarantees the statements stay out of the criminal case. Because Lybarger wasn't given that assurance, the Court found his termination, based in part on the compelled statements, couldn't stand.

Lybarger warnings are now standard practice in departments across California, and any officer ordered to answer questions in an internal affairs interview should confirm the warning has actually been given before answering. An interrogation that skips this step — or that blurs the line between a voluntary interview and a compelled one — can create serious problems for the department's ability to use anything said in it, in either a disciplinary or a criminal proceeding.
