A Federal Court Just Blew a Hole in California's Mask-Ban Law — But Don't Celebrate Yet If You're Not Federal

Legal News · Civil Rights · April 23, 2026

Legal News

In United States v. State of California, No. 26-926 (9th Cir., decided April 22, 2026), the Ninth Circuit did exactly that. Sacramento wrote a law meant to regulate face coverings and identification for federal immigration agents. A Ninth Circuit panel just told Sacramento it can't do that — and the ripple effects land squarely on California's own peace officers.

In United States v. State of California, No. 26-926, decided April 22, 2026, the court enjoined enforcement of Penal Code section 13654 — the identification-display mandate under the so-called No Vigilantes Act — against federal agencies and officers. The panel's reasoning is textbook Supremacy Clause: a state can't impose operational mandates on federal officers carrying out federal duties, full stop, no matter how the statute is dressed up as generally applicable.

Here's the twist nobody at the Capitol seems to want to talk about. The companion legislation — SB 627's ban on facial coverings for law enforcement — was written with the same severability clauses baked into both bills. That means even though the Ninth Circuit's reasoning guts these mandates as applied to federal officers, the restrictions, the criminal penalties, and the stripped civil immunities remain fully in force against California's own state and local officers.

Read that again: federal agents get out from under the law because of a constitutional doctrine that has nothing to do with California peace officers, while state and local officers — who had zero role in the federal immigration enforcement this legislation was ostensibly targeting — are stuck facing the exact restrictions the bill's own sponsor originally aimed at someone else. Officers who need face coverings for gang enforcement, riot response, or hazmat scenes are now operating under a "knowing and willful" liability standard that attaches to the act of covering your face, not to any intent to break the law — with statutory damages starting at $10,000 even without proof of actual harm.

This is exactly the outcome that was flagged in committee testimony before the bill passed. The law's core defect wasn't a drafting error the Legislature can quietly patch — it's a structural mismatch between who the bill was aimed at and who is actually going to eat the consequences.
