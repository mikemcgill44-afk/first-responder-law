Your Off-Duty Marijuana Use Is Legally Protected — Unless You Carry a Badge and a Personal Firearm

Legal News · Fitness for Duty · August 19, 2026

Legal News

California law now protects nearly every worker's right to use cannabis off the clock and off agency property, and lawmakers pointedly declined to carve out an exception for peace officers, dispatchers, jailers, or anyone else an agency might consider "safety sensitive." Under Assembly Bill 2188, codified at Government Code section 12954, it is unlawful for an employer to discriminate against a person in hiring, discipline, or any other term of employment based on their off-duty, off-premises cannabis use, or based on a drug test that detects only nonpsychoactive cannabis metabolites rather than active impairment. The statute carves out exceptions for building-and-construction-trade employees and positions requiring a federal security clearance — but conspicuously not for law enforcement.

That gap matters more than it might first appear. AB 2188 does let a "criminal justice agency" consider prior cannabis use if it surfaces in an applicant's criminal history background check, and agencies remain free to ask applicants about cannabis use or impairment that occurred on the job or at the worksite. But a drug screen that only detects nonpsychoactive metabolites — the kind that can linger in a person's system for weeks after use — can no longer be the basis for an adverse action against an off-duty employee. Agencies conducting marijuana testing must instead use a method that isolates active THC, and departments should not assume their existing zero-tolerance drug policy for sworn personnel survives this statute unchanged.

Here's the twist California's Legislature didn't have the power to erase: federal law still treats any "unlawful user" of a controlled substance, including marijuana, as prohibited from possessing a firearm under the federal Gun Control Act. That prohibition doesn't reach a firearm issued by and used exclusively for a government agency's own purposes, so an officer's agency-issued duty weapon is generally safe. But the exception does not extend to a personally owned firearm used to perform job duties — meaning an officer who uses their own personal weapon on the job, and who is a current marijuana user, may run headlong into a federal prohibition that AB 2188 cannot override, regardless of what California's anti-discrimination statute says about off-duty use.

Agencies should also remember that drug testing procedures for current employees remain a mandatory subject of bargaining under the Meyers-Milias-Brown Act, meaning any policy update in response to AB 2188 needs to go through the meet-and-confer process, not a unilateral memo. For officers, the practical takeaway is this: your off-duty use is now legally shielded from discipline in most circumstances, but if your duties involve carrying a personally owned firearm, federal law hasn't caught up to California's, and that gap is yours to navigate carefully.
