An Off-Duty Pursuit, A Fatal Crash, and Why the State Couldn't Just Walk Away

Legal News · Personal Injury · July 22, 2026

Legal News

A public agency can be held liable for its employee's conduct when that conduct occurs within the scope of employment and proximately causes injury to someone else. That principle, called respondeat superior and codified at Government Code section 815.2(a), usually gets litigated over things that happened on the clock — but a recent California Court of Appeal decision confirms it can also reach an off-duty peace officer's private car, if the facts about what he was doing and why are genuinely disputed.

A California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation officer was driving his own private vehicle to work early one morning when he decided two people at a gas station looked "suspicious." After some words were exchanged, the individuals drove off, and the officer pursued them onto the freeway in his personal car, driving recklessly and brandishing a firearm at them along the way. The pursuit ended in a crash at a freeway interchange: the car the officer was chasing caught fire, one occupant managed to self-extricate, and the other suffered catastrophic burns and injuries before dying more than a year later. The survivor and the deceased's family sued both the officer and CDCR.

The trial court granted CDCR summary judgment, ruling as a matter of law that the officer's conduct fell outside the scope of his employment — reasoning that made some intuitive sense, given he wasn't on shift and was driving his own car. The Court of Appeal reversed, in Adams v. Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, 2024 Cal. App. LEXIS 11, finding the conflicting evidence should have gone to a jury instead of being resolved on paper. The court pointed to specific unresolved factual disputes: the officer's actual CDCR duties involved supervising prisoners handling kitchen knives, not street-level law enforcement, cutting against an inference that a traffic pursuit was part of his job; and while the officer believed his clothing marked him as recognizable law enforcement, the record didn't establish whether the pursued individuals actually knew that before the crash.

For officers, correctional staff, and their families, the case cuts in a useful direction: an agency does not get to end a personal injury or wrongful death case at summary judgment just by pointing out an officer was off the clock and off agency property. Whether the scope-of-employment question ultimately favors the plaintiff, the officer, or the agency, this decision confirms it's a fact question for a jury far more often than public entities would like — which matters just as much to officers hoping their agency's coverage will extend to an off-duty incident as it does to the people injured by one.
