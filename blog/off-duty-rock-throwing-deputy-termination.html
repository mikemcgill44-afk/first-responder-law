He Threw a Rock at Her Car, Then Called Himself the Victim — His Termination Stood Anyway

Legal News · Discipline · August 24, 2026

Legal News

An off-duty deputy sheriff's decision to hurl a rock at a stranger's car has cost him his career, and his termination appeal shows just how little sympathy a disciplinary hearing officer has for an officer who frames his own bad judgment as self-defense. The case turned on a straightforward question any officer should be able to answer under pressure: did the response match the threat? Here, the hearing officer found it plainly did not, and upheld the termination under the department's own conduct-unbecoming standard, the same framework courts apply when reviewing discipline through a writ of administrative mandamus.

The deputy was dumping rocks and dirt in an open field while off duty when a civilian stopped to photograph him and warned she would post the dumping on social media. In response, the deputy threw a rock toward her car, striking it and causing significant damage. A separate law enforcement agency opened a criminal investigation into the civilian's allegations, and the deputy compounded his problems by never notifying his own supervisor that he was the subject of that investigation, a standalone policy violation. The department terminated him for conduct that discredited the agency, failure to report the investigation, and conduct unbecoming a deputy.

On appeal, the deputy claimed the civilian threw something at his vehicle first while his children sat inside, and that he threw the rock only to scare her away from his kids. The hearing officer wasn't persuaded: the deputy's own account placed the civilian already back inside her vehicle by the time he threw the rock, undercutting any claim of an imminent threat to his children. The deputy also argued he hadn't violated the Penal Code's illegal dumping statute because he was dumping dirt rather than garbage — an argument the hearing officer rejected outright, since the statute's dumping prohibition expressly covers rock and dirt. The hearing officer went further, calling the deputy's insistence that his conduct was justified "astounding," and reasoning that an officer who genuinely believes throwing a rock at a bystander's car was the right call is an officer likely to make the same call again.

The lesson for working officers is uncomfortable but simple: being recorded doing something you'd rather not have on camera does not entitle you to escalate against the person holding the phone. Departments and hearing officers alike will scrutinize the proportionality of an officer's reaction far more than the provocation that triggered it, and an after-the-fact self-defense narrative that doesn't match the officer's own timeline of events will do more damage to a case than the underlying incident ever could.
