Those Minutes Before Roll Call? The Feds Just Said They Count

Wage & Hour · June 8, 2024

Wage & Hour

A new U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division opinion letter makes that clear. Every shift has a shadow shift attached to it — the ten minutes checking gear before you clock in, the five minutes washing equipment after you clock out, the radio check that somehow never makes it onto the timesheet. Departments have spent years treating that shadow time as free. The U.S. Department of Labor just said, in writing, that it isn't.

A Letter From Washington With Real Teeth

The Department's Wage and Hour Division issued a formal opinion letter addressing one of the most common flashpoints in wage-and-hour disputes: work performed just before or just after a scheduled shift. The letter reached three conclusions that matter enormously for anyone who suits up before the clock starts running.

First, tasks that are genuinely integral to doing the job safely — donning protective gear, running equipment checks, sitting through pre-shift briefings — count as hours worked, period. Second, the fact that any single instance is brief doesn't make it disappear. Employers have long leaned on a legal doctrine that lets them ignore truly trivial, unpredictable slivers of time. The Department drew a hard line: recurring, predictable minutes that happen every single shift are not trivial just because they're short — they add up, and the law expects them to be paid. Third, rounding policies that quietly and consistently favor the employer, like always rounding an early clock-in back to the scheduled start time even though real work was already happening, don't pass muster either.

Why This Hits Close to Home for Public Safety

The letter arose from a hospital dispute, but its reasoning travels directly into fire stations and police departments. Pre-shift vehicle and equipment checks, briefings and shift-change information handoffs, and the report writing that spills past end of watch aren't incidental extras — they're essential to doing the job safely and are exactly the kind of task the letter says must be compensated. The same logic covers post-shift gear cleaning and storage. If your day genuinely can't start or end without a task, the law increasingly agrees that your pay shouldn't pretend otherwise.

What to Check at Your Own Department

This letter is a formal federal agency interpretation, and courts routinely give that kind of guidance real weight. If your department uses automated time clocks, "early arrival" buffers that quietly shave minutes off the clock, or an unspoken expectation that prep work happens "voluntarily" before the shift technically begins, it's worth taking a hard look at whether those practices line up with this guidance. Unpaid pre- and post-shift donning and doffing time, one-sided rounding practices, and the dismissal of recurring short tasks as too minor to matter are all now squarely in the crosshairs.
