California Just Made It a Crime for Officers to Hide Behind a Mask on Duty

Legal News · Discipline · June 23, 2026

Legal News

Concealing your face has long been a crime for civilians committing an offense in California, and lawmakers have now extended a version of that same logic to the officers enforcing the law. Senate Bill 627 makes it a misdemeanor for any local, state, or federal law enforcement officer to wear a facial covering that conceals their identity while performing their duties in California, with a narrow carve-out for SWAT teams actively engaged in tactical operations.

The statute isn't just a uniform rule enforced through internal discipline — it comes with a civil-liability trigger with real teeth. Any officer found to have committed certain enumerated torts while wearing a facial covering in knowing, willful violation of the law loses the ability to claim privilege or immunity in the resulting civil action, and becomes personally liable for actual damages or a statutory minimum of $10,000, whichever is greater. That's a meaningful departure from the qualified-immunity protections officers typically rely on, and it applies specifically to the subset of misconduct that happens while an officer's identity is deliberately obscured.

SB 627 also imposes an agency-level obligation: every law enforcement agency operating in California had to adopt and publicly post a written policy on facial coverings by July 1, 2026, addressing the minimum requirements the statute enumerates. That policy is presumed to comply with the law unless a member of the public files a verified written challenge, in which case the agency gets 90 days to fix any deficiency before the policy can be challenged in court. Until a court actually rules the policy noncompliant — and any appeals are exhausted — the agency's existing policy stays in effect.

For individual officers, the message is straightforward: whatever operational reasons might justify concealing your face during an ordinary patrol interaction, this statute treats that choice as carrying its own legal exposure if anything goes wrong during the encounter. Agencies should be walking every officer through the written policy now, not waiting for a challenge to surface a gap nobody noticed.
