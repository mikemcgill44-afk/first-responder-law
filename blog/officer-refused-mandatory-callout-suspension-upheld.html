He Refused a Mandatory Callout During the 2020 Protests — His 30-Day Suspension Just Got Upheld

Legal News · Discipline · August 6, 2026

Legal News

A police officer who refused a direct order to report for duty during a large-scale protest, choosing instead to guard his family's restaurant, has lost his appeal of the resulting 30-day unpaid suspension — and lost his claim that his captain's phone call about it violated the Public Safety Officers Procedural Bill of Rights Act (POBR) along the way. The case is a useful marker of just how far a department can go in ordering personnel to report during a civil disturbance, and how narrowly courts read POBR's interrogation protections when the conversation in question isn't actually about discipline.

In June 2020, in the aftermath of George Floyd's killing, a city's police chief issued a tactical alert ordering all sworn personnel to report for duty on the day of a large planned protest, absent contrary instruction from a supervisor. On the morning of the protest, an officer told his sergeant he would not report because he intended to protect his family's restaurant from nearby rioting. The sergeant reiterated that all sworn personnel were required to report under the tactical alert; the officer repeated that he was going to the restaurant instead. A captain then offered to arrange housing and safety for the officer's family so he could report for duty as ordered — an offer the officer declined, telling the captain he still intended to go protect the business himself.

The officer's disciplinary appeal board rejected his argument that his statement to the captain about protecting his family's business excused his failure to report, noting the department had already offered to protect his family and he had turned that offer down. He also argued the captain's phone call amounted to an improper POBR interrogation, since it touched on conduct that could lead to discipline. The board disagreed on two independent grounds: the captain never asked the officer any questions about a rule violation that could support discipline, and the captain ended the call immediately once the officer confirmed he was not reporting to work. With both defenses rejected, the board upheld the 30-day unpaid suspension, calling the officer's conduct one of the highest degrees of disloyalty an officer could display toward the department and the community it serves.

The POBR holding matters beyond this one case: a supervisor offering accommodations or simply confirming an employee's intentions is not automatically an "interrogation" that triggers POBR's procedural protections, even during a high-stakes conversation about reporting for a mandatory callout. If you are ordered to report during a civil disturbance and have a genuine, competing safety concern, the better move is to escalate that concern through the chain of command in writing and accept an offered accommodation rather than unilaterally deciding not to report — because a board reviewing the decision afterward will ask exactly that question.
