California Was One of Four States With No Way to Decertify a Bad Cop. That Changed — Permanently.

Legal News · Decertification · July 1, 2026

Legal News

For most of California's history, a peace officer fired from one department for serious misconduct could simply go get hired at the next one, because California had no statewide mechanism to revoke an officer's certification and bar them from the profession entirely. Until recently, California sat alongside Hawaii, New Jersey, and Rhode Island as one of only four states without any decertification process at all. Senate Bill 2, the Kenneth Ross Jr. Police Decertification Act, ended that streak by creating California's first permanent, statewide decertification system — and agencies that haven't updated their thinking around lateral hires are operating with an outdated picture of what's now possible.

Before SB 2, California peace officers already had to meet baseline standards under Government Code sections 1029 and 1031, including freedom from a disqualifying felony conviction and from bias based on race, gender, religion, disability, or sexual orientation. What was missing was any consequence for an officer who met those standards at hiring but violated them during their career. SB 2 fixed that gap by establishing the Peace Officer Standards Accountability Division within the Commission on Peace Officer Standards and Training, giving POST the authority to review agency investigations of serious misconduct and, where warranted, launch its own independent investigation — regardless of what an individual department's internal process concluded.

The mechanics matter for anyone facing an internal affairs investigation today. Individual agencies remain responsible for investigating misconduct allegations in the first instance, but POST now has the authority to review the investigative file and administrative appeal record for any officer facing a sustained finding of serious misconduct, and can independently investigate any officer who accumulates three separate allegations of serious misconduct within a five-year window, whether or not any single allegation was sustained by the employing agency. Where POST's review supports action, certification suspension or revocation proceeds through formal proceedings consistent with the Administrative Procedures Act — and a revoked certification follows the officer statewide, closing off the department-hopping option that used to make a bad termination survivable.

The practical shift for officers is significant: a serious misconduct finding is no longer just a fight over your job at one agency, it's potentially a fight over your entire career in California law enforcement. If you are the subject of an internal affairs investigation involving conduct that could qualify as "serious misconduct" — dishonesty, excessive force, bias, or abuse of power chief among them — treat the stakes as statewide from day one, because POST's accountability division now has a seat at that table whether your department invites them or not.
