One Missed Body-Cam Activation, Two Dishonesty Charges, and a Termination That Stuck

Legal News · Discipline · August 12, 2026

Legal News

A peace officer's termination for dishonesty was recently upheld on appeal after a body-worn camera failure and a disputed detention turned into a five-count disciplinary case that a city's Personnel Appeals Board found the officer simply could not talk his way out of. The case is a reminder that under most agencies' disciplinary schemes, a dishonesty finding — not the underlying use-of-force or detention decision itself — is often what actually ends a career, because dishonesty strikes at the credibility every future case an officer testifies in will depend on.

The incident began when the officer detained a man waiting across the street from a trolley station, on suspicion of smoking and fare evasion, even though the man was not standing on transit property and quickly turned out not to be smoking at all. When the man tried to walk away, the officer grabbed his shirt and pushed him into a seated position, then arrested him for allegedly assaulting the officer — a charge the officer added to the report after the man accused him of being unable to admit a mistake. The officer's body-worn camera was not activated until after he had already grabbed the man's shirt, but a bystander's video of the encounter was posted online and drew public protests before the department's own investigation was complete.

Following the investigation, the police chief terminated the officer on five separate grounds: two counts of dishonesty, including an allegation that he filed a false police report about the arrest, along with failure to comply with the department's body-worn camera policy, discourteous conduct toward the arrestee, and exceeding his authority by detaining the man without reasonable suspicion. On appeal, the officer argued he had reasonable suspicion because the man was standing on transit-owned property — an argument that fell apart when the officer admitted on the stand that he had quickly determined the man wasn't actually smoking, and a transit agency sergeant testified that no fare is required just to stand across the street from the platform. The city's Personnel Appeals Board upheld the termination in a unanimous vote.

The lesson for working officers isn't subtle: a body-worn camera activated late doesn't just create a policy violation, it creates a gap in the record that a disputed detention will fill with someone else's video — and once a board finds a dishonesty violation anywhere in the file, every other charge becomes far easier to sustain. If your camera activation, your report, or your account of an encounter is being second-guessed in an internal investigation, treat it with the seriousness of a termination case from day one, because that's often exactly what it becomes.
