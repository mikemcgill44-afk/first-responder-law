The City Tried to Skip the Bargaining Table on Your Pension. The Court Sent Back the Bill.

Pensions & Retirement · Labor Relations · August 3, 2023

Pensions & Retirement

A California Court of Appeal ruling on pension-reciprocity bargaining (Cal. Ct. App., 2023) makes the point unmistakably. Picture a pension system as a long, careful bridge that thousands of public employees quietly cross, year after year, on the promise that the planks were laid down properly and won't get pulled up without warning. In 2010, one California city decided a plank was too expensive to keep and yanked it — no announcement, no negotiation, just a vote by a retirement board and, a few years later, an ordinance. A California appellate court has now made clear that this is not how bridges, or pensions, are supposed to work.

The plank in question was reciprocity: for three decades, employees moving between the city's main pension system and its water-and-power retirement plan could carry their service credit with them. When the smaller plan's board decided the arrangement was draining its funds by roughly $183 million, it pulled the plug. The city later locked that decision into its municipal code, ending the practice of counting prior water-and-power service toward pension benefit calculations going forward.

A Union Coalition Says: Not So Fast

A coalition of unions representing the affected employees didn't dispute that the city could make this call unilaterally — retirement systems are allowed to redesign their own plumbing. What the coalition disputed was being frozen out of the conversation about what happens to the people standing on the bridge when a plank disappears. They demanded to bargain over the fallout: disability retirement calculations thrown off, retiree health subsidies disrupted, leave and transfer rights scrambled. The city handed over some paperwork but refused to sit down and actually negotiate.

The local employee relations board sided with the unions. It ordered the city to bargain over those downstream effects and, critically, to make employees financially whole for any pension losses suffered from the moment reciprocity ended until the parties either struck a deal or hit a genuine impasse. A trial court upheld that order and tacked on attorney's fees for good measure. The city appealed anyway, no longer contesting that it owed a bargaining duty, but insisting the remedy itself went too far.

The Appellate Court's Answer, in Three Parts

The Court of Appeal wasn't persuaded. First, it confirmed that the board's power to fashion "make whole" relief — essentially, back pay covering every dollar of pension and benefit loss — flows directly from the statute governing local labor relations for public agencies, the Meyers-Milias-Brown Act. Second, it held that this kind of remedy is exactly what effects bargaining violations call for: the employer bargains, and in the meantime, employees don't quietly eat the cost of a decision they never got a say in. Third, it rejected the idea that this somehow forces elected officials to reverse legislation. Ordering back pay is not the same as ordering a city council to rewrite an ordinance; it simply keeps the financial scoreboard even while the negotiating happens.

Here's the detail that matters most for anyone outside that particular city limits: the court grounded its whole analysis in the statewide labor relations statute, not in some quirk of local rules. That means the reasoning travels. Any California public employer that changes something affecting pensions — contribution formulas, reciprocity arrangements, service credit rules — without bargaining the effects still owes its employees a seat at the table, and potentially a check, until that bargaining happens.

What This Means If You're Standing on That Bridge

The lesson isn't "management can never touch pension mechanics without your permission." Employers often do retain the right to redesign a plan unilaterally. The lesson is that the moment such a change foreseeably ripples into your paycheck, your disability eligibility, or your retiree medical coverage, your union has a statutory right to demand a conversation about softening that landing — and a right to compensation if the employer stonewalls. If your agency has quietly changed anything pension-adjacent lately without picking up the phone, that silence may be exactly the kind of silence the law doesn't allow.
