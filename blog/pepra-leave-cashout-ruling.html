Banked Your Vacation Hours for Retirement? The California Supreme Court Just Redrew the Math.

Pensions & Retirement · July 30, 2025

Pensions & Retirement

The California Supreme Court’s PEPRA leave-cashout ruling (Cal. 2025) makes that point unmistakably. Every hour of vacation you didn't take was a small deposit into a jar you assumed you'd get to empty at retirement, dollar for dollar. On July 27, 2025, the California Supreme Court reached into that jar for legacy members of the state's county retirement systems and set a firmer lid on how much can come out at once.

The case, decided under the California Public Employees' Pension Reform Act of 2013 (PEPRA), asked a deceptively simple question: if your leave cashout is capped at a certain number of hours per calendar year, can you get around that cap by choosing a "final compensation" window that straddles two calendar years and effectively double-dips into two annual allowances? The Court's answer was no.

The Numbers That Started It All

The retiree at the center of the case accrued a hefty pile of leave every year and was permitted to cash out up to 200 hours in any single calendar year. He picked a twelve-month final compensation window that crossed a calendar-year boundary, then cashed out 40 hours in December of one year and another 200 hours in February of the next — 240 hours total, all falling inside that one window. His retirement system counted only 200 of them. The Supreme Court agreed with the system: the annual cap governs, no matter how cleverly the window is drawn.

The Most Interesting Part Isn't the Outcome — It's How the Court Got There

Here's where this case gets genuinely interesting for anyone who cares about how law actually gets made. The Court didn't claim the statute obviously required this result. It openly admitted the retirement system's reading "may not be the most immediately obvious or intuitive," calling it merely "plausible." Two lower courts had already flagged the same ambiguity. Faced with a genuinely unclear sentence, the Court didn't split the difference — it looked at why the Legislature wrote PEPRA in the first place (to stop pension "spiking," the practice of artificially inflating final pay right before retirement) and let that purpose settle the tie.

Chief Justice Guerrero, concurring, was refreshingly candid about the tension: the ordinary meaning of the statute's own words pointed the other way, she wrote, and the majority's reading survives only because the anti-spiking purpose was allowed to override the plain text once ambiguity opened the door.

Why Retirees Everywhere Should Care About a Ruling on Word Choice

For decades, California courts leaned toward favoring the pensioner whenever a retirement statute was genuinely ambiguous — ties went to the employee. This decision narrows that tiebreaker. The Court held that liberal construction in favor of pensioners "must be consistent with the clear language and purpose of the statute," and where an anti-spiking purpose is in play, that purpose wins even over a textually sympathetic reading. That's a meaningful shift in leverage, and it won't stay confined to leave cashouts. PEPRA is a long, hastily drafted statute full of provisions nobody has fully litigated yet — on-call pay, specialty premiums, equipment allowances. Expect retirement systems to invoke this ruling's reasoning any time they want to argue a category of pay shouldn't count.

What to Actually Do About It

If you're a legacy member planning to lean on a big leave cashout near retirement, the strategy of stretching your final compensation period across two calendar years to stack two annual caps no longer works. The annual limit set by your terms of employment now governs the entire twelve-month final compensation window, full stop. It's worth reviewing your retirement planning assumptions now, well before you file your paperwork, rather than discovering the shortfall on your first pension check.
