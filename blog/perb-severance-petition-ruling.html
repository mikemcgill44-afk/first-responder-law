Trying to Switch Unions Through the Back Door? PERB Just Nailed That Door Shut

Legal News · Labor Relations · June 3, 2026

Legal News

California’s Public Employment Relations Board closed that shortcut in two severance-petition rulings issued weeks apart (PERB, 2026). There's a shortcut some unions have tried when a specialized group of employees seems like a better fit somewhere else: file a "severance" petition to carve those employees out of their current bargaining unit, then quietly redirect them straight into an existing unit instead of forming something new. California's labor board just closed that shortcut for good, in two separate rulings issued weeks apart.

Two Cases, One Consistent Message

In the first case, a union initially sought to sever a small group of tradespeople from a larger unit, then amended the petition to instead slot those employees directly into a different union's existing bargaining unit. In the second, a deputy sheriffs' association tried something structurally similar — petitioning to pull two specialized classifications out of one union's units and drop them into its own existing unit, then, after the labor board's general counsel raised questions, amending course to propose a genuinely new standalone unit instead. In both cases, the incumbent unions fought back, and the labor board sided against the petitioners' attempt to get through amendment what the rules never allowed in the first place.

Why "Severance" Doesn't Mean What Some Unions Wish It Meant

The board's reasoning rests on the plain language of the regulations governing severance. A severance petition exists to create one thing and one thing only: a new, standalone bargaining unit made up exclusively of the employees being carved out. It is not, and was never designed to be, a mechanism for shuffling classifications into a completely different, already-established unit. The board was explicit that the regulatory language limiting a severed unit to those specific employees is a term of limitation, not an invitation to attach them somewhere else. That reading lines up with decades of precedent distinguishing severance from an entirely different procedure — unit modification — and with the underlying goal both procedures are supposed to serve: keeping established bargaining relationships stable rather than up for grabs.

The Timing Trap Hiding Inside Both Cases

Both decisions also drove home a second, easily overlooked point: severance petitions, and any amendments to them, have to land inside a narrow statutory window — a specific 29-day stretch tied to when the governing labor agreement is set to expire. A change that alters the fundamental nature of the petition, like switching from "join an existing unit" to "create a new one," isn't a minor technical fix that can slide in after the fact. It's a material change, and if it lands outside that window, it doesn't rescue the petition — it sinks it.

What This Means If Your Association Is Eyeing a Specialized Unit

Deputy sheriffs' associations, police officers' organizations, and similar groups often have good reason to think a specialized classification — institutional police, investigators, or similar roles — would be better served in its own dedicated unit. That instinct is still entirely valid after these rulings. What has to change is the execution: frame the petition from day one as creating a new, independent unit built solely around the employees being carved out, back it with a genuine community-of-interest analysis showing why that group's interests diverge from the larger unit, and file it squarely within the correct window period. If an initial petition turns out to be flawed, the better move is almost always to let it be dismissed and refile cleanly at the next opportunity, rather than trying to patch a fatal defect with a late amendment.
