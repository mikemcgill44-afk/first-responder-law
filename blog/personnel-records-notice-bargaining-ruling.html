Before Your Personnel File Goes Public, Shouldn't Someone Have to Tell You?

Legal News · Labor Relations · June 5, 2026

Legal News

Transparency laws opened up officer personnel records in ways that would have been unthinkable a generation ago. Nobody seriously disputes that the disclosure mandate itself exists and has to be followed. The question a New York labor board just answered is a different one entirely: when an agency decides how to implement that mandate — timing, notice, a chance to flag an error before records go out the door — does the union get a say?

New York's Answer: Yes, Emphatically

After New York repealed the law that had long shielded police disciplinary records, unions demanded to bargain over the mechanics of releasing them: notify the affected officer when a request comes in, hand over copies of what's about to be disclosed, and give the officer a real chance to object before anything goes public. The municipalities refused, arguing the whole subject was off-limits to bargaining and that negotiating would blow through statutory response deadlines. New York's labor board didn't just disagree — it affirmed a violation in full.

The board's reasoning drew a clean line: the decision to comply with a disclosure law isn't negotiable, but how an agency implements that decision very much is. Pre-disclosure notice and review procedures fall squarely within that implementation bucket. There was no evidence such procedures would blow any deadline, and the board was candid about why it mattered — record disclosure has a direct, serious impact on officers' reputations, safety, and future employment prospects, and that impact is exactly what triggers a duty to bargain over reasonable protective measures.

Why a New York Case Belongs on Every California Union's Radar

California's own disclosure framework under Penal Code section 832.7 has expanded significantly through recent legislation, opening far more of the personnel file to public request than existed before. California's labor relations statute for public agencies imposes the same basic duty New York's board just enforced: employers must meet and confer in good faith over the effects of a nonnegotiable statutory mandate on employees' terms and conditions of employment. Deciding what gets redacted, when officers get notified, and how disclosures get timed are exactly the kind of discretionary implementation choices that duty is built to cover. Advance notice and a genuine chance to be heard let officers flag inaccuracies or push for appropriate redactions — without slowing down or blocking the transparency the underlying law requires.

What Your Association Should Do With This

Any time a department adopts or revises how it handles these kinds of disclosure requests, that's the moment to formally demand to bargain over the pre-disclosure procedure — not after the fact. A refusal to bargain at that point may itself amount to an unfair labor practice. And once you're at the table, a memorandum of understanding is the right vehicle to lock in negotiated protections: real advance notice, a genuine opportunity to be heard, and a documented process before anything sensitive goes out the door.
