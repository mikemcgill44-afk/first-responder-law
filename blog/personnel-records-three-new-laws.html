Three New Laws Just Redrew the Line Around Your Personnel File

Legal News · Civil Rights · August 14, 2026

Legal News

Confidential peace officer personnel records have never been an absolute vault — the Government and Penal Codes have long carved out exceptions for the California Public Records Act and for investigations by a grand jury, a district attorney, the Attorney General, or POST. Three new statutes just widened those exceptions further, while also closing off a practice some agencies and officers had quietly relied on for years. Together, Assembly Bills 847, 1178, and 1388, all amending Penal Code section 832.7, reset the rules governing who can see your file and what your department is allowed to promise you about it.

AB 847 expands access to confidential personnel records to a civilian oversight board or commission for a law enforcement agency, and separately authorizes an inspector general and sheriff oversight board members to access confidential records needed to perform their oversight duties — provided they maintain the same confidentiality the records were already subject to. A sheriff oversight board may review these records in closed session, so long as the closed session itself complies with applicable confidentiality law. For officers under an agency with civilian oversight, this means one more category of people now has a lawful path into your file.

AB 1178 cuts the other direction, at least for officers working undercover. When a court is deciding whether to order disclosure of confidential personnel records in response to a Public Records Act request, the statute now requires the court to specifically consider whether the officer works undercover and whether their duties demand anonymity, in evaluating whether disclosure would pose a significant, articulable danger to that officer's physical safety. Where that danger exists, the law enforcement agency may redact the record rather than produce it in full.

AB 1388 targets a different problem entirely: side agreements that quietly make misconduct disappear. The statute prohibits an agency from entering into any agreement with a peace officer that would require the agency to destroy, remove, or conceal a misconduct investigation record, halt or predetermine the outcome of an investigation, or restrict disclosure of an allegation or investigation. Any contract term that violates this rule is now void and unenforceable as a matter of public policy — and, in a pointed twist, the prohibited agreement itself becomes subject to disclosure under a Public Records Act request. Departments and officers who have historically negotiated "clean record" separation terms need to revisit those agreements now, because the version that used to be standard practice may no longer survive a records request, let alone a court's review.
