Prop 36 Gave Officers New Felony Arrest Power for Theft and Drug Crimes. Two Years In, Here's What Changed.

Legal News · Criminal Law · August 1, 2026

Legal News

Passed with 68% voter support, Proposition 36 (the Homelessness, Drug Addiction, and Theft Reduction Act, eff. Dec. 18, 2024) rolled back significant portions of the charging limitations that had constrained officers responding to retail theft and drug offenses since 2014. Two years in, the numbers show just how much that rollback has been used.

Approximately 40,000 new felony arrests for drug and theft offenses have followed in the measure's wake, driven by stricter consequences for repeat theft offenders, smash-and-grab retail crime, and organized theft rings — categories that had previously been difficult to charge as felonies absent aggravating factors. The measure also requires convicted fentanyl and hard-drug dealers to receive formal warning that a future sale resulting in death can expose them to homicide-level liability.

The rollout hasn't been without friction. County-level implementation of the treatment-mandated felony provisions has varied widely, and funding shortfalls have meant the treatment side of the measure is lagging well behind the arrest side — with far more people facing felony prosecution than completing the treatment diversion the measure promised as an alternative.

For patrol officers, the practical shift is real: conduct that would have been a cite-and-release misdemeanor before December 2024 may now support a felony arrest, particularly for repeat offenders. Make sure your department's charging guidance reflects the current thresholds, since the gap between old and new practice is still tripping up some agencies.
