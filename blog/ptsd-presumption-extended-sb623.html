The PTSD Presumption for Cops and Firefighters Was Set to Expire — Lawmakers Bought It Four More Years

Legal News · Workers' Compensation · June 28, 2026

Legal News

The workers' compensation presumption that has quietly protected thousands of California first responders with post-traumatic stress was written with an expiration date, and that date was closing fast. Under Labor Code section 3212.15, as it stood before this year, certain state and local firefighting personnel and peace officers benefited from a rule that treats post-traumatic stress developing during their service as a work-related injury by disputable presumption — meaning the burden shifts to the employer to prove the PTSD did not arise from the job, rather than the officer or firefighter having to prove that it did. That presumption entitles the injured member to full hospital, surgical, and medical treatment, disability indemnity, and death benefits. But the provision was set to sunset on January 1, 2025.

Senate Bill 623 pushes that sunset date back to January 1, 2029, keeping the presumption alive for four additional years rather than letting it lapse and forcing every affected peace officer and firefighter back onto the ordinary, much harder burden of proving a PTSD diagnosis was caused by their work. The bill also requires the Commission on Health and Safety and Workers' Compensation to report back to the Legislature on how effective the presumption has actually been, and specifically to study claims filed by categories of employees who are not currently covered by it — including public safety dispatchers, whose work is every bit as trauma-exposed as sworn personnel's but who have historically been left out of these presumption statutes.

For members currently receiving treatment, or considering filing a PTSD claim, the practical upshot is straightforward: the disputable presumption remains available through the extended window, and a claim doesn't need to independently prove work-causation from scratch the way a claim from an employee outside the presumption's coverage would. That matters because PTSD claims are notoriously difficult to litigate without a presumption, given how hard it is to isolate a single work incident as the medical cause of a condition that often develops cumulatively.

The dispatcher study built into SB 623 is also worth watching. If the Commission's report supports extending presumption coverage to dispatchers, that would represent a meaningful expansion of who gets the benefit of the doubt in a PTSD claim — and it's exactly the kind of legislative groundwork that tends to precede a future bill actually making that change.
