A Bill Meant to Rein In Federal Agents Could Leave California Officers Holding the Bag

Legal News · Legislation · June 11, 2026

Legal News

Legislation aimed at one target doesn't always land only on that target. Senate Bill 747, dubbed the "No Kings Act," was written to address concerns about overreach by federal agents operating in California. Nobody testifying against it disputes that goal. The problem, according to advocates for California's own peace officers, is that the bill's current wording could quietly rewrite the rules for every state and local officer in California too — and not in their favor.

The One Sentence Doing All the Damage

SB 747 creates a new state-law claim modeled on federal civil rights litigation under Section 1983. That much is uncontroversial. The trouble is a single phrase: the bill says a defendant "may assert qualified immunity," full stop — without tying that defense to the decades of federal case law that actually define what qualified immunity means and how it works. Left as written, California could end up with two different versions of qualified immunity running in parallel: the well-established federal standard, and a brand-new, undefined California standard that nobody has litigated yet.

Representatives for California's peace officers association and the state's police chiefs association didn't mince words at a recent Assembly Judiciary Committee hearing. One put it plainly: the concern isn't accountability, it's "creating a brand new California cause of action that introduces significant legal uncertainty." Another warned bluntly against ending up with "a watered down dual definition of qualified immunity."

Why a Vague Standard Is Worse Than a Strict One

Officers train on the federal standard. Departments build policy around it. Courts have spent decades refining what it means for an officer's conduct to be shielded by qualified immunity, case by case, fact pattern by fact pattern. A brand-new California-only version, left undefined, doesn't give officers more protection or less — it gives them uncertainty, which in split-second, high-stakes moments is arguably the most dangerous thing you can hand someone. One committee member floated the idea of defining the state standard around "ordinary circumstances" rather than tying it to existing federal law, which is precisely the kind of drift that worries officer advocates most: two courts, two standards, two different answers to the same set of facts arising from the same incident.

A Fix That's Already on the Table

The proposed amendment is narrow and specific: expressly tie the state qualified immunity defense to the same federal standards and precedent that already govern Section 1983 claims. That single change eliminates the risk of conflicting rulings on the same incident, spares agencies from having to retrain an entire workforce on a second, undefined standard, and preserves the predictability officers rely on when they have to make a decision in real time, not in a law library.

Notably, several lawmakers on the committee — from both sides of the underlying policy debate — have signaled openness to exactly this kind of clarification, describing it as a fixable problem rather than a dealbreaker. That's a meaningful opening, and one worth watching closely as the bill moves forward.

What's at Stake If It Doesn't Get Fixed

Without the amendment, roughly 80,000 California officers could be left making critical decisions without a clear sense of what standard will actually govern their liability exposure afterward. Litigation costs for local governments — and by extension, taxpayers — would likely climb. Recruitment and retention, already a strain across the profession, would face one more headwind. None of that serves the accountability goals the bill's own supporters say they want.
