Every "Serious Misconduct" Allegation Now Goes to the State — Here's What Counts

Legal News · Discipline · August 18, 2026

Legal News

Since January 1, 2023, an internal complaint against a California peace officer isn't just an internal matter anymore. Under Senate Bill 2 and the statute it created, Penal Code section 13510.8, every law enforcement agency in the state must report all allegations of "serious misconduct" by a peace officer to the Commission on Peace Officer Standards and Training's Peace Officer Standards Accountability Division — the body created to review agency investigations and, where warranted, launch its own investigation into conduct that could support suspension or revocation of an officer's certification. That reporting duty attaches at the allegation stage, not after a sustained finding, and POST's own FAQ page is blunt about it: "there are no statutory exceptions for frivolous, unfounded, or exonerated allegations."

Penal Code section 13510.8 and its implementing regulation, Commission Regulation 1205, define "serious misconduct" broadly enough to sweep in most of what internal affairs actually investigates: dishonesty connected to reporting, investigating, or prosecuting a crime or officer misconduct; abuse of power, including intimidating witnesses, knowingly securing a false confession, or knowingly making a false arrest; physical abuse, including excessive or unreasonable force; sexual assault as defined in Penal Code section 832.7(b), which extends to conduct between members of the same agency; demonstrating bias based on race, national origin, religion, gender identity, housing status, sexual orientation, or disability; law-violating conduct egregious or repeated enough to be inconsistent with an officer's duty to uphold the law; participating in a law enforcement gang; failing to cooperate with a misconduct investigation; and failing to intercede when present for another officer's excessive use of force.

The one category still being fought over is bias. On December 29, 2023, POST published a Notice of Proposed Regulatory Action to expand the definition of "demonstrating bias" under Commission Regulation 1205, after concluding the existing text and Penal Code section 13510.8 needed additional language spelling out what actually counts. The proposed language would deem an officer to be demonstrating bias when words, actions, or other conduct would lead a reasonable person to conclude the officer has not fairly and impartially performed — or will not fairly and impartially perform — their law enforcement duties. Public comment closed on February 12, 2024, and the amendment has been sitting with the Office of Administrative Law since.

For officers, the practical effect is that a complaint your department might once have logged and closed quietly can no longer stay quiet if it falls into one of these categories — your agency is legally obligated to send it up the chain to POST regardless of how the internal investigation ultimately shakes out. If you're the subject of an internal affairs investigation involving any of these categories, understand that the file isn't staying inside your department, and get counsel involved from the start rather than after POST has already opened its own file.
