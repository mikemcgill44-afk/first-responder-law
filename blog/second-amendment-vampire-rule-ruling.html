The Supreme Court Just Staked Hawaii's "Vampire Rule" Through the Heart — California Should Take Notice

Legal News · Second Amendment · June 26, 2026

Legal News

Nicknames don't usually attach themselves to gun laws, but Hawaii earned one. Its rule flipped the ordinary logic of private property open to the public on its head: instead of "come on in unless we say otherwise," it told anyone carrying a licensed concealed firearm the opposite — "stay out unless we say otherwise." Critics called it the "Vampire Rule," because like the old folklore about vampires needing an invitation to cross a threshold, a permit holder needed the property owner's express, affirmative welcome before they could walk in armed. The Supreme Court just drove a stake through it, 6-3.

A Law That Inverted a Centuries-Old Default

For as long as there's been a common law, the default rule for property open to the public has been simple: everyone's welcome unless the owner says no. Hawaii carved out a single exception to that welcome mat — people exercising a specific constitutional right. The Court held that flipping the presumption solely for concealed-carry permit holders imposes a real, modern burden on the right to bear arms, one with no comparable footing in American legal history.

Hawaii tried to find that footing anyway, reaching for old statutes to serve as historical analogues. The Court wasn't impressed. Colonial poaching laws addressed a completely different problem. A single 1893 Oregon statute, adopted more than a century after the Second Amendment, told the Court almost nothing about the right's original meaning. And when Hawaii reached for an 1865 Louisiana "Black Code" statute — a law engineered to keep newly freed Black Americans disarmed and defenseless — the Court didn't just decline to use it. The opinion pointedly refused to treat a "tainted artifact" of that history as evidence of constitutional tradition, calling back to the Court's own recognition that the right to keep and bear arms mattered urgently to vulnerable Black citizens in the Reconstruction South who had no one else to protect them.

Why This Isn't Just Hawaii's Problem

California has been defending its own sweeping restrictions on where concealed-carry permit holders may actually carry — restrictions broad enough that critics have said they hollow out the permit exactly where people most need it. There was a real possibility California could preserve those restrictions by remodeling them after Hawaii's approach, which a lower appellate court had blessed. That escape route just closed. With the Supreme Court rejecting Hawaii's model outright, there's no version of it left standing for California to copy.

A Quiet Win for Officers, Too

There's a second, less obvious beneficiary here: sworn officers, including retirees, who have run into a narrower reading of self-defense rights in use-of-force litigation. Earlier appellate precedent had suggested there's no standalone constitutional right to self-defense outside the home for ordinary citizens, a reading that courts leaned on to uphold restrictive use-of-force policies for officers under a lower level of scrutiny. This ruling reaffirms that the right to carry for self-defense extends into public spaces and private property open to the public — undercutting the reasoning behind that narrower approach and giving officers firmer constitutional ground to challenge restrictive policies governing their own right to carry, on duty or off.

The Takeaway

The historical test the Court applied here is being enforced with real teeth, not treated as a box to check. States that want to restrict carry rights in ordinary public and quasi-public spaces need actual historical tradition behind them — not a repackaged version of a law the Court has now rejected, and certainly not artifacts of laws built to disarm and oppress. Litigation will continue, particularly in California-heavy circuits, but the ground just shifted meaningfully in favor of permit holders, sworn officers, and retirees alike.
