Monday-Morning Quarterbacking a Life-or-Death Decision: Why One Appeals Court Finally Said Enough

Legal News · Use of Force · July 14, 2026

Legal News

There's a particular kind of courtroom theater that plays out whenever an officer's split-second decision gets dissected months later under fluorescent lights, with all the time in the world and none of the danger. A prosecutor stands at a podium and asks, calmly, why the officer didn't simply wait, or take the stairs, or try something else. It's an easy question to ask from a podium. It is not the question the law actually requires jurors to answer.

A Texas appellate court just said so, loudly, and reversed a conviction because of it. The case doesn't bind California courts, but its reasoning tracks California law so closely that it's worth every officer's attention.

Elevator Doors Open. Everything Happens at Once.

Four officers responded to a report that a man was wandering a downtown building with a knife to his own throat, threatening suicide, with a known history of mental health crises. When the elevator doors opened on the floor where he was last seen, he was facing a mirror, blade at his neck. He turned. The knife turned with him — now pointed outward, toward the officers, in a space with nowhere to retreat. He advanced, ignoring shouted commands. The officers fired. He died, and one officer was convicted of a firearms offense and sent to prison, despite fellow officers testifying they'd have done the same thing, an expert calling the force justified, and the department's own investigators clearing the shooting.

The appellate court threw the conviction out entirely, entering a judgment of acquittal. Its reasoning is worth sitting with: prosecutors built their case almost entirely on what the officers supposedly should have done differently — wait, use the stairs, try less-lethal options. The court didn't buy it. "Could have," "would have," and "should have," the court wrote, do not disprove that a use of force was justified. A person advancing with a weapon while ignoring commands isn't demonstrating compliance just because no blow has landed yet, and officers don't have to absorb the first strike before they're allowed to respond.

California Already Has This Exact Rule — And It's Not Going Anywhere

California officers already operate under nearly identical protection, anchored in both the U.S. Constitution and state statute. The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Graham v. Connor requires that force be judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on scene, not with "the 20/20 vision of hindsight," and it expressly accounts for the reality that officers must make split-second calls in tense, uncertain, rapidly evolving circumstances. California's Penal Code section 835a codifies the same idea: reasonableness is measured by the totality of circumstances known to the officer at the time, not by a calmer reconstruction assembled afterward. An officer doesn't have to retreat, doesn't become "the aggressor," and doesn't lose the right of self-defense by using objectively reasonable force.

California courts have consistently protected this standard from erosion. They've made clear an officer isn't required to pick the single most cautious option available, only to act within the range of what's reasonable under the circumstances — and that where deadly force is otherwise justified, there's no separate constitutional requirement to try less-lethal alternatives first.

Why This Fight Matters Beyond One Verdict

Earlier legislative proposals in California would have pushed things much further than any of this, requiring that deadly force be used only when "strictly necessary" and when "no reasonable alternative" existed — a standard the Ninth Circuit has already called a demand for "superhuman judgment." That standard would have invited exactly the kind of hindsight second-guessing the Texas court just rejected: a factfinder convicting an officer any time some other path could later be imagined, whether or not it was realistic in the moment. Those provisions were stripped out before the law was finalized, preserving the reasonable-officer standard that actually governs today.

The bottom line for officers and the departments that stand behind them: your split-second call gets judged as a split-second call, not against a script written by someone who had all the time in the world to write it. A subject who advances with a weapon while disregarding commands isn't asking to be treated as compliant, prior information that someone is in crisis doesn't freeze the threat assessment as the encounter evolves, and there's no separate duty to have picked the most cautious tool in the box.
