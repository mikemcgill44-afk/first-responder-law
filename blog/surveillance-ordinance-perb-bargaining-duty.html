The County Rolled Out a Surveillance Ordinance Without Bargaining — PERB Said That's Not How This Works

Legal News · Meet & Confer · May 14, 2026

Legal News

A county can decide, on its own, to regulate how its departments use surveillance technology. What it cannot do is adopt new rules that dump workload, safety exposure, and even criminal liability onto the deputies who have to follow them without ever sitting down to bargain the consequences. That is the core holding of County of Santa Clara, PERB Decision No. 2799-M (2021), a Public Employment Relations Board decision that every California law enforcement labor association should have on file.

Santa Clara County's Board of Supervisors began developing an ordinance in 2014 to govern county-owned surveillance technology — drone cameras, automated license plate readers, GPS trackers — requiring departments to submit an impact report and use policy before deploying any new equipment, and making intentional misuse of the technology a criminal misdemeanor. When the county's deputy sheriffs' association caught wind of the proposal in 2016, it demanded to meet and confer over both the ordinance and its effects on association members, flagging concerns about vague definitions of "surveillance technology," increased workload from new reporting requirements, and a particularly sharp worry: that requiring advance public notice of what surveillance technology would be deployed and where could compromise officer safety. The county met with the association once, the parties reached an impasse, and the Board of Supervisors adopted the ordinance unchanged a month later.

The association filed an unfair practice charge, and PERB's General Counsel issued a complaint alleging the county violated the Meyers-Milias-Brown Act by adopting the ordinance without completing negotiations over the decision or its effects. An administrative law judge split the difference, finding the county had no duty to bargain the underlying decision to adopt the ordinance itself, but did have a duty to bargain its effects — and had failed to do so in good faith, particularly regarding the consequences employees would face for violating the new rules. PERB agreed, emphasizing that even when an employer has no obligation to bargain a managerial decision, it must still give notice and a genuine opportunity to meet and confer over the decision's reasonably foreseeable effects on matters within the scope of representation. Because the county never responded to the association's proposed alternatives on the misconduct-consequences issue, PERB found bad-faith effects bargaining and ordered the county to bargain those effects and to hold off enforcing the ordinance against represented employees until the parties reach agreement, bargain to a good-faith impasse, or the association abandons the effort.

The decision is a genuinely useful roadmap for any association facing a new department policy dressed up as a pure "managerial decision." PERB sorted managerial choices into three buckets: decisions with only an indirect, attenuated effect on employment — advertising, product design, financing — which are not mandatory bargaining subjects at all; decisions directly defining core terms like wages and hours, which plainly are; and decisions like this one, sitting closer to the employer's retained management authority but still carrying direct, foreseeable effects on workload, safety, and discipline exposure for represented employees. If your department rolls out a new surveillance program, technology deployment, or reporting mandate and your association wasn't given a real opportunity to bargain its effects on you, this decision is the standard your representative should be citing.
