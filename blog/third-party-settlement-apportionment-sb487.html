Hurt on the Job by Someone Else's Negligence? California Just Rewrote Who Gets Paid First

Legal News · Personal Injury · May 12, 2026

Legal News

When a peace officer or firefighter is injured on duty because of a negligent third party — a drunk driver who hits a patrol car, a property owner whose unsafe structure collapses on a firefighter — California law has long let the employer step in and recover its workers' compensation costs directly from that third party's insurance. The problem was that the employer's reimbursement claim could eat into the same limited insurance pool the injured officer or firefighter needed to actually be made whole. Senate Bill 487 now puts a ceiling on how much of that pool the employer can take.

Under SB 487, an employer's claim against a third-party defendant's liability insurance is capped at one-third of the available policy limits — but only once the officer or firefighter shows that their total damages exceed what's left over after the employer's claim is satisfied, and that the total insurance available isn't enough to fully cover both the employer's reimbursement and the employee's own damages. In other words, the cap kicks in specifically in the scenario where there isn't enough insurance money to go around and the officer or firefighter would otherwise be squeezed out.

The law goes further than just capping the employer's take from the settlement pool. It also limits the employer's right to reimbursement, subrogation, or lien to that same one-third threshold, and explicitly bars the employer from clawing back the difference by asserting a credit or offset against the employee's future workers' compensation benefits. Any settlement or release in one of these cases is now invalid unless it spells out that the employer won't take more than the one-third cap and won't offset future benefits — meaning defense counsel and claims adjusters need to rebuild their standard settlement language around this requirement.

For officers and firefighters navigating a third-party injury claim, this is a meaningful change in leverage: your recovery is no longer automatically subordinated to your employer's reimbursement interest when the available insurance can't cover both. For agencies and their risk managers, it means revisiting how settlement negotiations and lien recovery are structured whenever an employee's on-duty injury involves a negligent outside party.
