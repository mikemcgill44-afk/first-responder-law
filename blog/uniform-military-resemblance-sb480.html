Your Uniform Could Now Be a Misdemeanor Waiting to Happen — Here's the Two-of-Three Rule

Legal News · Discipline · August 3, 2026

Legal News

A law enforcement uniform that looks a little too much like a soldier's is no longer just a public-relations problem in California — it's a compliance issue with a criminal penalty attached. Under Senate Bill 480, codified at Penal Code section 13655, any agency other than the Department of Fish and Wildlife that employs peace officers is barred from authorizing its employees to wear a uniform "substantially similar" to one used by the armed forces or a state militia, and separately barred from allowing camouflage-print uniforms altogether.

The statute defines "substantially similar" functionally: a uniform crosses the line if it resembles a military or militia uniform closely enough that an ordinary person might mistake the wearer for a service member. But SB 480 also builds in a safe harbor. A uniform is not considered substantially similar to military dress if it includes at least two of three specific features: a badge or star mounted on the chest, a sleeve patch displaying the employing agency's insignia, or the word "Police" or "Sheriff" prominently displayed across the back or chest. Agencies that keep at least two of those three markers in place should be in the clear on the substantial-similarity question, even for otherwise tactical-looking gear.

The prohibitions apply specifically to officers assigned to uniformed patrol, uniformed crime suppression, or uniformed duty at any event — including protests, demonstrations, and similar disturbances, which is precisely the context that prompted the bill in the first place. Notably, SB 480 exempts members of a SWAT team, sniper team, or tactical team while they are engaged in an actual tactical response or operation, recognizing that specialized units sometimes need gear the statute would otherwise restrict.

For agencies, this is a uniform-closet audit, not just a policy memo: pull your patrol, crowd-control, and crime-suppression uniforms and confirm each one carries at least two of the three safe-harbor markers, and separately confirm no camouflage pattern has crept into everyday patrol wear. For officers, if your department issues you gear that doesn't clearly display a badge, an agency patch, or agency lettering, that's a question worth raising with your supervisor before you're the one wearing it on a call that draws public attention.
