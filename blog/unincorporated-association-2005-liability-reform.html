Your Association Doesn't Have to Incorporate Anymore — Here's the Law That Changed Everything

Legal News · Collective Bargaining · August 26, 2026

Legal News

For decades, the advice to any new police or fire association was automatic: incorporate, because an unincorporated association was a legally fragile thing to be. It had no clean identity apart from its members, which meant individual members could potentially be on the hook for the association's debts, and the association itself often couldn't sue, be sued, or hold property in its own name without workarounds. California quietly erased most of that risk when it enacted the Uniform Unincorporated Nonprofit Association Act, Corporations Code sections 18000 through 24001.5, operative January 1, 2005 — and a lot of boards are still making the incorporation decision as if that law never happened.

Under Corporations Code section 18605, a member, director, officer, or agent of an unincorporated association "is not liable for a debt, obligation, or liability of the association solely by reason of being a member, director, officer, or agent." The association itself now answers for its own conduct "to the same extent as if the association were a natural person" under section 18250 — ordinary entity liability, the same allocation of risk a corporation provides. Code of Civil Procedure section 369.5 lets an unincorporated association sue and be sued in its own name, ending the old joinder headache where litigation against the group risked becoming litigation against every individual member. And Corporations Code sections 18100 through 18135 let the association hold bank accounts, sign contracts, and own real property in its own name, just like a corporation. The specific legal exposure that used to justify incorporating almost automatically now exists for unincorporated associations too.

That doesn't mean incorporating lost all its advantages. Banks, insurers, landlords, and vendors are, in practice, often simply more comfortable with a corporation than an unincorporated association, whatever the statute books say. If the association ever dissolves or merges, a nonprofit mutual benefit corporation can distribute its remaining funds directly to members under Corporations Code section 7411 — a real advantage for an association that has built up a significant treasury or legal defense fund, and one a public benefit corporation typically doesn't get, since those generally must dedicate remaining assets to another tax-exempt purpose rather than the membership. A corporation also carries a far deeper, more heavily tested body of case law behind it than the comparatively young Title 3 framework governing unincorporated associations.

Here's the tradeoff that doesn't get talked about enough: incorporating isn't a one-time decision, it's a recurring obligation, and the failure mode is ugly. A California nonprofit corporation must file a Statement of Information with the Secretary of State every two years under Corporations Code section 8210 and stay current with the Franchise Tax Board. Miss either one, and under Revenue and Taxation Code section 23301 the corporation is suspended or forfeited — which means it loses its power to sue, defend a lawsuit, or enforce a contract until it's revived. For an association that's routinely in grievance arbitration, unfair practice charges, or duty-of-fair-representation litigation, that isn't paperwork housekeeping. It's a real risk that can surface at the worst possible moment, mid-negotiation or mid-litigation. An unincorporated association has no equivalent state suspension mechanism; its main ongoing exposure runs instead to federal tax law, where failing to file a required Form 990 for three consecutive years triggers automatic loss of federal tax-exempt status under Internal Revenue Code section 6033(j) — and that risk applies to incorporated and unincorporated associations alike, so it isn't a point of comparison between the two structures.

Tax-exempt status itself shouldn't tip the scale either way. Labor organizations typically qualify under Internal Revenue Code section 501(c)(5), and the IRS accepts exemption applications from unincorporated associations and corporations equally; the parallel California exemption works the same way. Recognition as a bargaining representative under the applicable public-sector labor relations statute likewise turns on employee support and employee-organization status, not corporate form. Strip those factors out, and what's left is a genuine judgment call: for an association with a modest treasury, no real estate, and no significant investment portfolio, staying unincorporated is often the lower-maintenance, lower-risk choice under current law. For an association sitting on significant property or a substantial legal defense fund, or a board that wants the more established liability shield despite the compliance burden, incorporating as a nonprofit mutual benefit corporation is worth a serious look — provided the board actually keeps up with the filings that come with it.
