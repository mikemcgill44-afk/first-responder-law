Refuse the Surgery, Lose the Disability Retirement — A California Court Just Confirmed the Rule

Legal News · Line-of-Duty Disability · August 20, 2026

Legal News

A Ventura County sheriff's deputy spent four years turning down a back surgery three separate doctors recommended, and it cost him his service-connected disability retirement. Under the common-law doctrine of avoidable consequences — sometimes called mitigation of damages — a county retirement board can lawfully deny disability retirement benefits if the member's own unreasonable refusal of recommended medical treatment caused, continued, or worsened the very disability keeping them off the job. That is exactly what a California appellate court just confirmed in Mendoza v. Board of Retirement of the Ventura County Employees' Retirement Association, 2025 Cal. App. LEXIS 865, and the ruling applies to every County Employees Retirement Law system in the state, not just Ventura's.

The deputy injured his back on the job in 2014 and again in 2015. Three different doctors recommended surgery, and one predicted a 90% chance of a good to excellent outcome. He declined it anyway, telling one examining physician he was "simply scared" based on things he'd read online and heard from friends. He applied for a service-connected disability retirement in 2016, and the county fought the application from the start. Over the next several years he also stopped a doctor-ordered home exercise program because it hurt, and declined to participate in a work-hardening program a later doctor recommended to rebuild his lifting capacity. At his 2019 administrative hearing he called no witnesses and testified that his symptoms had improved on their own — testimony the hearing officer did not find persuasive.

The hearing officer recommended denial on four independent grounds: the refused surgery, the abandoned home exercise program, the skipped work-hardening program, and a general failure to pursue reasonable treatment. The county's retirement board adopted that recommendation, and the superior court denied the deputy's petition for a writ of administrative mandate. On appeal, he argued the doctrine of avoidable consequences shouldn't apply because so much time had passed that the original surgery would no longer even be effective. The Court of Appeal rejected that argument and then some — holding that the doctrine reaches not only cases where treatment would still work today, but also cases where the member could have returned to work years ago but for an unreasonable refusal to timely accept treatment that has since become less effective purely because of the passage of time. In plain terms: you cannot run out the clock on your own recovery window and then point to the closed window as your excuse.

The lesson here isn't "never say no to a doctor." It's that a bare refusal, unsupported by a documented medical reason, a credible second opinion, or a genuine contraindication, will not survive scrutiny by a retirement board or a reviewing court — and a pattern of refusals across surgery, home exercise, and work-hardening will look even worse than any single decision standing alone. If you are weighing whether to accept a recommended procedure or rehab program while a disability retirement application is pending, get a second opinion in writing and put your reasoning on the record before you decline, not after the board has already ruled against you.
