The County Didn't Have to Be Cruel — Just Wrong. That Was Enough to Lose.

Legal News · Fitness for Duty · February 25, 2016

Legal News

Stanislaus County Deputy Sheriff Dennis Wallace injured his knee, worked light duty in the property and evidence room for months under documented medical restrictions, and was then pulled off the job entirely and placed on unpaid leave for roughly two years based on the County's reading of his medical reports. When Wallace finally passed a fitness-for-duty exam and returned to patrol, he sued for disability discrimination — and at trial, the jury was told he had to prove the County acted out of animus or ill will toward him. In Wallace v. County of Stanislaus (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 109, the Court of Appeal reversed, holding that instruction was wrong as a matter of law.

Relying on the California Supreme Court's decision in Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, the Fifth District held that a disabled employee only has to show that an actual or perceived disability was a "substantial motivating factor" in the employer's adverse decision — not that the employer harbored any hostility. The court was explicit about what that means in practice: California's statutory scheme protects employees from an employer's honest, good-faith, even reasonable mistake about their physical condition. If the employer gets it wrong and the employee pays the price in lost duty and lost pay, the employer bears that cost, not the employee.

The court went further and found, as a matter of law on the existing record, that the County's decision to sideline Wallace was in fact based on its mistaken belief he couldn't safely perform a deputy's essential functions — meaning the only issue left for retrial was how much Wallace was owed in damages, not whether the County was liable at all. The case was defended by Liebert Cassidy Whitmore on the County's behalf, and the opinion is a useful read for exactly the audience that firm otherwise represents: it shows how a fitness-for-duty determination can expose an agency to liability even when nobody did anything in bad faith.

For officers and firefighters navigating a fitness-for-duty process after an injury, Wallace is a reminder that "the department wasn't trying to be unfair" is not a defense if the underlying medical assessment was simply incorrect and cost you time on the job. Documentation — clear written restrictions from your treating physician, delivered promptly and in writing — is what turns a good-faith dispute into a case you can actually win.
