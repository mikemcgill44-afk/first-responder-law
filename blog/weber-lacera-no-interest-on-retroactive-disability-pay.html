LACERA Made You Wait Years for Your Disability Pension — Turns Out It Doesn't Owe You Interest

Legal News · County Retirement · April 10, 1998

Legal News

Katherine Weber, a Los Angeles County Superior Court hearing referee injured in an on-duty car accident, waited 59 months for LACERA's Board of Retirement to finally approve her service-connected disability retirement. When the Board paid her a lump sum of $48,455.51 to cover the retroactive benefits she was owed, she asked for interest on top of it. LACERA said no. In Weber v. Board of Retirement, the Court of Appeal agreed with LACERA — and closed the door on interest claims for members stuck waiting through slow-moving disability determinations under the County Employees Retirement Law (CERL).

Weber's co-plaintiff, Laura Garnica, a Sheriff's Department intermediate typist clerk, had a similar story: over a year fighting an initial denial before LACERA's Board granted her service-connected retirement and paid her $16,791.37 in back benefits. Both women argued that Civil Code section 3287, which allows interest on damages "capable of being made certain by calculation," should apply to the lump sums LACERA owed them for the months and years they went without their full pension.

The court, relying on the California Supreme Court's AFL-CIO decision in the unemployment insurance context, held that an administrative agency like LACERA's Board can only award interest if a statute expressly or implicitly gives it that power — and CERL doesn't. The court drew a sharp line: interest under section 3287 is available when a court orders LACERA to pay benefits it wrongfully denied, but not when the Board itself grants benefits after an internal review process that simply took a long time. Because Weber and Garnica were never denied their benefits — just delayed — the court found there was no "wrongful withholding" to compensate for.

The practical lesson for LACERA members has not changed since 1998: a slow eligibility determination, however frustrating, is not the same thing as a wrongful denial, and the retroactive lump sum you eventually receive will not include interest for the agency's own delay. If your application is denied outright and you have to go to court to reverse that decision, interest becomes available in a way it never was for Weber and Garnica — which makes the distinction between "slow" and "denied" worth understanding before you decide how to respond to a stalled claim.
